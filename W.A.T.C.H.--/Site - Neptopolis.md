Player Experience & Core Loop Overview

---
1. What Is This Game?
W.A.T.C.H. Site: Neptopolis is a first-person VR/Flatscreen exploration mystery set in a submerged research and containment site built around the ruins of an ancient drowned city.
The player is sent into Neptopolis on short, dangerous expeditions. There is no combat. The primary challenge is deciding what to investigate, how far to push deeper into the site, and when to turn back before the expedition becomes unsustainable.
The central resource is oxygen. Every action has a cost, so exploration becomes a constant negotiation between:
> **What do I want to learn, and how much of my remaining time am I willing to spend learning it?**
The deeper the player goes, the more they discover about both the ancient city and the W.A.T.C.H. operation that was built on top of it.
Most importantly, Neptopolis remembers previous expeditions.
A failed or incomplete expedition is therefore not simply a restart. The player's knowledge, discoveries, and actions gradually change what is possible on the next visit.
---
2. The Player Fantasy
The intended fantasy is:
> **I am an investigator entering a place that should have remained buried. I have limited time to understand what happened here, and the place itself seems to know that I am coming back.**
The player should feel like they are:
Exploring somewhere forbidden.
Physically present inside an ancient, drowned world.
Solving mysteries through observation rather than combat.
Making meaningful decisions under resource pressure.
Gradually becoming familiar with an initially incomprehensible place.
Realising that the environment is responding to them.
The player should not primarily feel like a soldier, monster hunter, or traditional survival-horror protagonist.
---
3. Core Experience Principles
3.1 Exploration Over Combat
There are no weapons and no conventional enemy encounters.
The game gets its tension from:
Oxygen depletion.
Environmental instability.
Locked or inaccessible areas.
Uncertainty about what lies deeper within the site.
The risk of spending too much time investigating.
The consequences of choosing one route over another.
The environment itself is the obstacle.
---
3.2 Curiosity Is the Primary Driver
The player should constantly encounter questions that encourage one more room, one more console, one more inscription, one more investigation.
Examples:
Why is this W.A.T.C.H. chamber built around an ancient structure?
Why was this room deliberately sealed?
Who was the researcher represented in this recording?
Why does this symbol appear in places the player has never visited?
Why did the facility's internal architecture change?
Why does a previously dead mechanism now respond to the player?
The game continually creates knowledge hunger.
---
3.3 Short Runs, Long Mystery
A single expedition should be relatively short — approximately 20–35 minutes as a target.
The short run length is important because it creates a repeatable rhythm:
> **Go in → discover → make decisions → push your luck → escape or fail → learn → go back.**
A player's overall journey is therefore much longer than an individual run.
The campaign is built from many short expeditions that collectively reveal one larger mystery.
---
3.4 The Environment Remembers
The defining feature of the game is that the world is not static between runs.
Neptopolis is gradually revealed to possess some form of memory.
This memory can manifest through:
Changed room configurations.
Newly accessible passages.
Previously inert mechanisms activating.
Different versions of rooms.
Environmental growth or deterioration.
Messages or symbols appearing after previous actions.
Echoes of past events.
Subtle traces of previous player behaviour.
The player should eventually begin to ask:
> **Is the labyrinth changing because I am discovering it — or is it changing because it is discovering me?**
---
4. The World From the Player's Perspective
The Surface
The player begins outside the main site.
They know only the broad facts:
Neptopolis is a rediscovered ancient city.
W.A.T.C.H. established a classified facility around it.
Something happened at the site.
Communications and/or the original expedition were lost.
The player's objective is to enter, investigate, and retrieve enough information to understand what happened.
The player is equipped for underwater exploration and given a finite oxygen supply.
The descent itself establishes scale and isolation.
---
5. A Typical Expedition — Player Perspective
The following is the intended experience of a normal run.
---
5.1 Preparation
The player chooses or is assigned an objective for the expedition.
This could be something like:
Investigate a specific W.A.T.C.H. sector.
Recover an important research record.
Locate a missing researcher.
Confirm whether a containment chamber is still active.
Reach a newly discovered Neptan structure.
Retrieve evidence relating to a previous expedition.
The objective gives the expedition a reason to exist beyond simply wandering around.
The player checks their equipment, confirms their oxygen supply, and begins the descent.
---
5.2 Entering Neptopolis
The player reaches the submerged complex and enters through a known access point.
The first areas are likely to be comparatively familiar W.A.T.C.H. spaces:
Pressure locks.
Operations rooms.
Laboratories.
Storage areas.
Containment chambers.
Maintenance corridors.
The player immediately begins interacting with physical objects.
They may:
Open doors.
Read displays.
Inspect equipment.
Activate machinery.
Examine markings.
Collect tools.
Record discoveries.
The site feels abandoned but not empty.
---
6. The Core Loop
The fundamental loop is:
Enter → Explore → Investigate → Spend Oxygen → Discover → Decide → Escape → Remember → Return
Each part matters.
---
6.1 Enter
Start a new expedition with:
A goal.
A limited oxygen supply.
A set of tools/equipment.
Knowledge from previous expeditions.
---
6.2 Explore
Move through the site.
The player's route is not completely predetermined.
Rooms and connections can vary between expeditions while remaining curated rather than purely random.
The player may encounter:
Familiar spaces.
Alternative versions of familiar spaces.
Previously inaccessible areas.
New routes.
Unexpected transitions into ancient Neptopolis.
---
6.3 Investigate
The player physically examines the environment.
Investigation should feel tactile in VR.
Examples:
Turn a valve.
Open a drawer.
Align a strange mechanism.
Hold an object up to a scanner.
Activate a pressure plate.
Trace a symbol.
Reconstruct a damaged recording.
Operate an ancient device.
Examine a biological sample.
Investigation consumes oxygen because time and effort are the game's fundamental currency.
---
6.4 Discover
Successful investigation provides:
Clues.
Story information.
Access codes.
New locations.
Tools.
Environmental changes.
Knowledge about characters.
Knowledge about Neptopolis.
Knowledge about W.A.T.C.H.
Discoveries are intended to feel like genuine progress even when the current expedition ends.
---
6.5 Decide
The player is constantly choosing between competing priorities.
For example:
> "I have enough oxygen to leave safely, but I have not investigated the lower chamber."
Or:
> "The route back is long, but this mechanism may unlock something important."
Or:
> "I can spend oxygen trying to understand this room, or I can push deeper while I still have enough reserve."
This is where oxygen creates tension without requiring combat.
---
6.6 Escape
The player eventually chooses to leave, or is forced to leave because oxygen is critically low.
Escaping ends the active expedition.
The player returns to the surface with:
Knowledge.
Evidence.
Recovered artefacts or tools.
An updated understanding of the site.
Potentially new information that changes the next expedition.
---
6.7 Remember
The site state changes.
The player may notice:
A newly opened route.
A room in a different state.
A new symbol.
A changed recording.
A mechanism reacting differently.
A memory echo.
The game communicates that the expedition mattered.
---
6.8 Return
The player begins another expedition with more knowledge and a slightly different world.
The player's accumulated understanding becomes the most important form of progression.
---
7. Oxygen as the Central Mechanic
Oxygen should function as much more than a health bar.
It represents:
Time.
Effort.
Risk.
Choice.
Opportunity.
Every meaningful action should therefore carry an implicit question:
> **Is this worth the oxygen?**
This creates tension naturally because there is no need for a visible enemy to create urgency.
---
8. What Happens When Oxygen Gets Low?
The player's physical and sensory experience should progressively change.
Potential effects include:
Faster breathing.
Increased heartbeat.
Visor fogging.
Reduced visual clarity.
Audio distortion.
Reduced environmental awareness.
Changes in haptics.
Increasingly unreliable perception.
The final phase can be a Last Gasp state inspired by the structure of One Breath Left.
The purpose is not simply to punish the player.
It creates a memorable final push:
> **Do I risk going deeper when I may only have a few actions left?**
---
9. Rooms as Narrative Units
The original One Breath Left design demonstrates the value of treating rooms as more than geography. Its Navigation Deck separates rooms from their descriptions and mechanics, while individual rooms contain investigation milestones, clues, events, and special rules. fileciteturn0file0 fileciteturn0file1
For Neptopolis, this philosophy translates directly into VR.
Each room should function as a small authored narrative system.
A room can contain:
A visual identity.
A reason to exist.
One or more physical interactions.
A narrative question.
One or more discoveries.
A potential state change.
A relationship with another room.
The player should rarely enter a room and think:
> "This is just another corridor."
Instead:
> "There is something here I haven't understood yet."
---
10. Example of a Room Experience
W.A.T.C.H. Containment Laboratory
On first visit
The player enters a dark laboratory.
A containment chamber dominates the centre of the room. Its interior is empty, but condensation suggests something was inside recently.
A dead terminal sits beside it.
A maintenance panel is partially open.
The player can investigate these elements independently.
Investigation 1
The player restores power to the terminal.
They discover a partial W.A.T.C.H. report:
> "Subject demonstrates response to mnemonic stimuli."
The player learns that W.A.T.C.H. was not merely studying the ruins.
They were experimenting with something that interacted with memory.
Investigation 2
The player opens the maintenance panel.
Inside is an unfamiliar ancient mechanism integrated into the W.A.T.C.H. machinery.
Interacting with it causes the room lighting to shift.
For several seconds, the laboratory appears as it looked decades earlier.
A researcher walks through the room.
The player cannot interact with them.
The scene ends.
Later Run
The player returns.
The containment chamber is no longer empty.
Nothing is visibly inside it.
But a handprint appears against the interior glass when the player approaches.
The room has remembered.

11. The Three Visual Layers
The environment is defined by three overlapping eras.
Layer 1 — Ancient Neptopolis
Stone.
Mosaics.
Glyphs.
Ritual spaces.
Tide-glass.
Ancient architecture.
Layer 2 — Neptan Arcane Technology
Luminous conduits.
Bio-magical systems.
Memory devices.
Strange mechanisms.
Living or semi-living architecture.
Layer 3 — W.A.T.C.H.
Steel.
Industrial equipment.
Containment hardware.
Research stations.
Security systems.
Modern scientific infrastructure.
The visual identity comes from these layers physically colliding.
Example:
> A W.A.T.C.H. server room has been constructed inside an ancient Neptan archive. Bioluminescent roots have grown through the server racks, connecting themselves to the facility's cables.
---
12. How the Mystery Unfolds
The story should not be delivered as one linear exposition dump.
Instead, the player assembles it from fragments.
There are potentially several mysteries running simultaneously.
Mystery A — What happened to W.A.T.C.H.?
Why was the site abandoned?
---
Mystery B — What happened to Neptopolis?
Why did this city disappear beneath the ocean?
---
Mystery C — What was W.A.T.C.H. studying?
What exactly did they discover beneath the ruins?
---
Mystery D — Why is the architecture changing?
Why does the site rearrange itself?
---
Mystery E — Why does Neptopolis remember the player?
This becomes the deepest mystery.
---
13. How a Campaign Might Unfold
The following is an example of the emotional progression rather than a locked storyline.
Early Expeditions — "Something Went Wrong"
The player believes they are investigating a lost research site.
The mystery is primarily W.A.T.C.H.-focused.
The environment is understandable.
---
Middle Expeditions — "This Place Is Older Than We Thought"
Ancient Neptopolis becomes increasingly visible beneath the facility.
The player realises that W.A.T.C.H. built its operation around something much older and stranger than originally assumed.
---
Later Expeditions — "The City Is Active"
The site begins responding directly to the player's actions.
Routes and rooms change.
Memory echoes become more precise.
The player begins to suspect that the city is not simply replaying the past.
It may be learning.
---
Endgame — "The City Knows You"
The player's accumulated knowledge finally allows access to the deepest areas.
The final mystery is no longer simply:
> "What happened here?"
It becomes:
> **"Why was I allowed to come back?"**
The exact truth and ending remain a future design decision.
---
14. What Makes a Run Feel Meaningful?
A successful run does not need to end with the player reaching a major objective.
A run is successful when the player leaves with something that changes their understanding of the world.
For example:
"I now know who the missing researcher was."
"I discovered why this chamber was sealed."
"I found the symbol that matches the ruins below."
"I learned how to access the flooded archive."
"I now know why W.A.T.C.H. was afraid of the city."
This makes failure valuable.
---
15. Failure Is Progress
If the player runs out of oxygen or otherwise fails an expedition, the game should avoid making the result feel like wasted time.
Instead:
Knowledge is retained.
Story discoveries remain unlocked.
World state may change.
The next expedition can build upon the previous one.
The player should eventually think:
> "That expedition went badly, but I learned something important."
This is essential to the short-run structure.
---
16. VR Interaction Philosophy
VR should not simply be the camera through which the game is viewed.
Physical interaction is part of the investigation fantasy.
The player should routinely use their hands to:
Open.
Pull.
Turn.
Insert.
Align.
Examine.
Scan.
Carry.
Place.
Activate.
The goal is to make investigating Neptopolis feel like physically solving a mystery inside a real place.
---

Neptopolis can retain this philosophy while converting the tools into physical VR equipment.
Potential examples:
Spectral / Mnemonic Scanner
Reveals traces of past events.
Ancient Resonance Probe
Interacts with Neptan mechanisms.
W.A.T.C.H. Multi-tool
Opens, repairs, or bypasses damaged equipment.
Lumen Filter
Makes hidden symbols or biological structures visible.
Memory Recorder
Allows the player to capture certain environmental echoes.
The important rule is that tools should expand investigation possibilities, not become weapons.

19. The Most Important Design Relationship
Everything should reinforce one central relationship:
> **Curiosity versus oxygen.**
The game should constantly tempt the player to spend more time than they can safely afford.
That is where the tension comes from.
Not:
> "Something is chasing me."
But:
> "I could leave right now. But I need to know what is behind that door."
That distinction is central to the game's identity.
---
20. The Intended Player Experience in One Sentence
> **Enter a drowned world, investigate its mysteries under relentless oxygen pressure, escape with what you have learned, and return to discover that the place remembers you.**

21. Design DNA from One Breath Left
The original One Breath Left is a major inspiration for the structure of Neptopolis.
Its particularly relevant ideas include:
Exploration organised around rooms.
Oxygen as the central limiting resource.
Investigation tracks and milestones.
Tools that modify the basic rules.
Modular room construction.
Different scenarios changing the same underlying exploration framework.
An explicit epilogue after the main exploration phase. fileciteturn0file0
The uploaded Navigation Deck also demonstrates a physical deck structure containing standard rooms, non-standard rooms, corridors, and item cards — a useful model for translating a curated modular environment into a digital system. fileciteturn1file0

This document intentionally does not lock several major creative decisions yet.

What was the city built for?

23. Prototype Priority
The first prototype does not need the complete story.
It needs to prove the experience.
The first playable slice should ideally contain:
A small underwater arrival sequence.
A W.A.T.C.H. entrance area.
Three to five connected rooms.
A working oxygen economy.
Physical VR investigation.
One meaningful narrative mystery.
One room that changes after an action.
An escape state.
A second visit where the player recognises that the site has changed.
The critical question for the prototype is:
> **Does it feel compelling to enter the same place twice when the second visit reveals that the place remembers what happened during the first?**
If the answer is yes, the rest of the game can be built outward from that foundation.
---
24. Development Philosophy
The game should be developed from player experience → system → content, rather than starting with a huge world map or lore bible.
Recommended order:
Step 1 — Prove the expedition loop
Enter → explore → spend oxygen → investigate → escape.
Step 2 — Prove one room
Make one room genuinely satisfying to investigate in VR.
Step 3 — Prove the mystery loop
Ensure discoveries create meaningful questions.
Step 4 — Prove persistence
Change one room between runs based on player actions.
Step 5 — Expand the room vocabulary
Introduce more room archetypes and connections.
Step 6 — Build the narrative framework
Layer W.A.T.C.H., Neptopolis, and the deeper mystery onto the proven systems.
---
25. Current North Star
The project should always be measured against this experience:
> **You enter a beautiful, ancient, impossible place.**
>
> **You have only a limited amount of time to understand it.**
>
> **Every discovery raises a more interesting question.**
>
> **You leave before you understand everything.**
>
> **When you come back, the place has changed — because it remembers you.**
That is the experience we are trying to build.
