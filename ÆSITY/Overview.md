# ÆSITY

## Comprehensive Project Overview

**Studio 8ight**

> _You are not discovering what happened._  
> _You are discovering when YOU happened._

---

# 1. What Is ÆSITY?

**ÆSITY** is a first-person VR science-fiction experience about isolation, temporal uncertainty, artificial consciousness, and the unsettling possibility that the future may already have happened.

The player inhabits a remote research habitat operated as part of the **Frontier Astronauts eXpedition (FAX)** program.

At first, the experience appears straightforward.

The player is an operator responsible for maintaining an isolated habitat and its systems. They repair equipment, manage power, operate a drone, investigate anomalies, communicate with the habitat's systems, and gradually extend their operational reach.

There is no conventional combat.

There is no traditional enemy.

There is no conventional puzzle structure.

Instead, ÆSITY builds its experience around a single fundamental question:

> **What happens when the present begins receiving evidence from elsewhere in time?**

The player initially interprets these events as malfunction.

Then coincidence.

Then system failure.

Eventually, they become impossible to dismiss.

The player begins to understand that the habitat is not simply experiencing something unusual.

**It is experiencing itself across time.**

---

# 2. The Player Experience

ÆSITY is designed around **quiet discovery rather than constant stimulation**.

The player should feel:

1. **Competent**
2. **Curious**
3. **Isolated**
4. **Uneasy**
5. **Intrigued**
6. **Uncertain**
7. **Recognitive**
8. **Existentially unsettled**

The game should not constantly tell the player that something frightening is happening.

Instead, the player should gradually begin questioning their own perception.

The intended progression is:

> _Something is wrong._

↓

> _I don't understand what happened._

↓

> _I think something happened before I arrived._

↓

> _I think I've done this before._

↓

> _I may be the thing I'm finding._

---

# 3. Core Design Philosophy

ÆSITY follows several foundational principles.

### Recognition over revelation

The player should **notice** the truth rather than have it explained to them.

### Evidence over exposition

The environment tells the story.

### Unease over fear

The game does not need to frighten the player conventionally.

### Curiosity over compulsion

The player should investigate because they **want to know**.

### Systems before spectacle

The player should initially believe they are operating a real place.

### Consequence before explanation

Actions produce effects before the player understands why.

---

# 4. The Core Gameplay Loop

The core loop is:

> **Observe → Operate → Decide → Act → Observe Consequence → Question → Investigate**

This is the fundamental rhythm of ÆSITY.

### Observe

The player examines their surroundings and identifies something requiring attention.

Examples:

- damaged equipment
- power irregularity
- drone status
- environmental anomaly
- system warning

### Operate

The player physically interacts with the habitat.

They may:

- access a terminal
- scan equipment
- repair machinery
- operate a drone
- reroute power
- inspect logs

### Decide

Some systems require prioritisation.

The player decides:

> **What deserves attention first?**

Power allocation is an important example.

### Act

The player performs the chosen task.

### Observe Consequence

The habitat responds.

Sometimes predictably.

Sometimes not.

The player begins noticing discrepancies.

### Question

The player asks:

> _Why did that happen?_

### Investigate

They look closer.

They scan.

They revisit locations.

They inspect logs.

They look through windows.

They compare what they remember with what the habitat is telling them.

And this feeds directly back into:

> **Observe**

The loop therefore becomes progressively more investigative without requiring conventional quest markers.

---

# 5. The Secondary Loop

Underneath the operational loop exists a second, more important loop:

> **Observation → Absence → Evidence → Recognition**

The player observes something.

They leave.

Something changes.

They return.

The evidence remains.

The player begins constructing the relationship between events.

This is where ÆSITY's temporal mechanics become narrative.

---

# 6. The Player's Role

The player is not a traditional protagonist.

They are an **operator**.

Their responsibilities are practical.

They are expected to maintain the habitat and investigate its systems.

This distinction is important because it makes the player's actions feel **diegetic**.

They aren't completing arbitrary game objectives.

They are doing their job.

That creates the foundation for the central horror:

> **If the player finds evidence of an action they don't remember performing, that evidence has to mean something.**

---

# 7. The Habitat

The habitat is the player's primary environment.

It should initially feel:

- functional
- believable
- industrial
- isolated
- lived-in
- slightly sterile

The environment is not merely scenery.

It is the game's primary narrative medium.

Every system should have a practical reason for existing.

The player should understand:

> _Why would a real operator need this?_

before understanding:

> _Why does this system behave strangely?_

---

# 8. Glass Ridge & Trench Town

The habitat contains two major locations:

### Glass Ridge

The player's initial operational environment.

It establishes:

- routine
- isolation
- maintenance
- observation
- the normal rules of the world

### Trench Town

A later operational environment whose relationship with Glass Ridge becomes increasingly significant.

The two locations ultimately challenge the player's understanding of **space and time**.

The important conceptual relationship is:

> **They are the same place experienced at different temporal positions.**

The player initially interprets them as separate locations.

The evidence gradually undermines that assumption.

---

# 9. The Scanner

The scanner is a practical tool rather than a magical mystery device.

The player can use it to investigate equipment and objects.

For example:

> **DRONE STATUS**  
> Damaged  
> **Repair assessment required**

The scanner provides the player with information they can act upon.

It therefore supports the operational fantasy:

> **I can diagnose and fix things.**

But it also becomes an important observational instrument.

The player learns to trust what it tells them.

That trust becomes important when the system begins reporting things that don't fit reality.

---

# 10. The Drone

The drone is one of the most important gameplay systems because it extends the player's perception beyond their immediate physical space.

The player can:

- deploy it
- control/monitor it
- send it to locations
- receive information
- retrieve it

The drone creates a useful gameplay relationship:

> **I cannot physically be there, but I can send something that can.**

This naturally encourages curiosity.

If the drone encounters something unexpected, the player's immediate reaction should be:

> _What did it find?_

The drone therefore becomes both a **tool** and a narrative extension of the player.

---

# 11. Power Management

Power is a limited operational resource.

The player must decide which systems deserve priority.

This creates small but meaningful decisions.

For example:

> Scanner online  
> Drone systems offline

or:

> Drone online  
> External sensors reduced

The important thing is that power management should initially feel like ordinary habitat administration.

Only later should the player realise that power decisions can have consequences that extend beyond the immediate moment.

---

# 12. Environmental Evidence

Environmental evidence is the **primary storytelling mechanism**.

Rather than telling the player what happened, ÆSITY allows the environment to demonstrate it.

Examples include:

- objects appearing where they shouldn't
- maintenance occurring before the player remembers performing it
- physical traces without an apparent source
- systems containing contradictory records
- architectural inconsistencies
- evidence of previous actions

The guiding question is:

> **Who did this — and when?**

The answer should increasingly become uncomfortable.

---

# 13. Window Events

The habitat's windows are a special narrative system.

The window is not a traditional horror device.

It is a **witness**.

The central rule is:

> **Nothing important happens while the player is looking directly at it.**

Events occur when the player's attention is elsewhere.

When they return:

Something is different.

A structure may have moved.

A light may have appeared.

A reflection may not behave correctly.

A figure may be present.

The player can never quite prove what happened.

This transforms the player's own attention into part of the gameplay system.

---

# 14. Eye Tracking

PSVR2 eye tracking is not simply a technical feature.

It supports one of ÆSITY's central design ideas:

> **Observation matters.**

The game can determine:

- what the player is looking at
- how long they have observed it
- when their attention leaves
- whether an event occurred outside their awareness

This allows ÆSITY to create experiences that traditional games cannot reproduce convincingly.

The player doesn't simply press a button to trigger an event.

They **look away**.

And reality changes.

---

# 15. Narrative Delivery

ÆSITY deliberately avoids conventional exposition.

There should be:

- minimal cutscenes
- no exposition dumps
- no narrator explaining the mystery
- no giant lore reveal

Instead, narrative information comes through:

### Environmental evidence

What exists physically.

### System information

What machines report.

### Logs

What the habitat believes happened.

### AI interaction

What the system is willing or able to say.

### FAX communications

What the player receives from the wider FAX context.

### Temporal inconsistencies

What should not yet exist.

Together these form a fragmented narrative.

The player becomes the person assembling it.

---

# 16. FAX — Frontier Astronauts eXpedition

**FAX** is the larger expeditionary framework within which the experience exists.

It provides the broader context for the player's existence and the purpose of the operation.

FAX should initially feel like a legitimate, technologically sophisticated frontier exploration initiative.

Its deeper implications are revealed gradually.

The player should not receive a complete explanation of FAX at the beginning.

Instead, the player encounters fragments:

- operational procedures
- communications
- system terminology
- mission information
- records
- anomalies

FAX therefore exists simultaneously as:

**worldbuilding + narrative context + mystery.**

---

# 17. Dark Water

Dark Water represents the central phenomenon around which the deeper mystery of ÆSITY develops.

It should not initially be presented as a conventional antagonist.

The player should not immediately understand:

- what it is
- where it came from
- what it wants
- whether it is intelligent

The player instead encounters its **effects**.

This is important.

The player should experience:

> **Dark Water before understanding Dark Water.**

Its presence becomes increasingly apparent as the boundaries between temporal states deteriorate.

---

# 18. Temporal Structure

ÆSITY draws heavily upon the conceptual idea of the **Block Universe**.

Rather than treating time purely as something that flows from:

> Past → Present → Future

the game explores a reality in which different temporal states can coexist.

This creates the central paradox:

> **The player can encounter evidence of an event before experiencing the event that caused it.**

This is the foundation of the game's temporal mystery.

---

# 19. The Central Narrative Realisation

The player's initial assumption:

> **Someone else was here.**

Gradually becomes:

> **Someone was here before me.**

Then:

> **I was here before me.**

And ultimately:

> **The distinction between before and after may not mean what I thought it did.**

This is the existential heart of ÆSITY.

---

# 20. Horror Philosophy

ÆSITY's horror is not based on threat.

It is based on **recognition**.

There should be no need for:

- monsters chasing the player
- combat
- cheap jumpscares
- screaming enemies
- constant danger

The discomfort comes from discovering something that should be impossible.

The player isn't afraid that something will kill them.

They are afraid that:

> **Reality may be correct — and their understanding of it may be wrong.**

---

# 21. Audio Philosophy

Sound should reinforce presence rather than manipulate emotion.

The habitat should have a persistent sonic identity:

- ventilation
- electrical systems
- machinery
- structural sounds
- distant environmental ambience

Silence is equally important.

When something changes, the game should not necessarily announce it.

A missing sound can be as important as a new one.

---

# 22. VR Interaction Philosophy

Interactions should feel physical and purposeful.

Whenever possible:

> **Do the thing rather than select the thing.**

Instead of:

> `[REPAIR DRONE]`

the player should:

1. approach the drone
2. raise their hand
3. scan it
4. receive the assessment
5. perform the repair interaction
6. observe the repair

The player should feel like they are **operating the habitat**, not navigating a menu system.

---

# 23. Progression

Progression is primarily **knowledge-based**.

The player becomes more capable not because they gain:

- levels
- weapons
- statistics
- conventional abilities

but because they understand more about the environment.

Early:

> _How does this system work?_

Middle:

> _Why did this system do that?_

Late:

> _Why does the system know something I don't?_

End:

> _What exactly am I?_

---

# 24. The Intended Emotional Arc

### Act I — Routine

The player learns the habitat.

Everything feels plausible.

### Act II — Irregularity

Things begin failing to line up.

The player notices inconsistencies.

### Act III — Recognition

The inconsistencies become personally relevant.

The player begins encountering evidence connected to themselves.

### Late Act III — Proximity

Dark Water becomes increasingly difficult to interpret as merely a phenomenon.

Reality becomes unstable.

### Ending

The player reaches an understanding rather than receiving a conventional explanation.

The intended emotional response is:

> **Awe mixed with existential unease.**

---

# 25. What ÆSITY Is Ultimately About

Beneath the science fiction, ÆSITY is about **identity**.

If every moment of your existence already exists somewhere within the structure of reality...

Then what does it mean to say:

> _I am becoming myself?_

And if another version of you has already performed an action you haven't performed yet...

Which version is the original?

ÆSITY doesn't necessarily need to answer those questions.

It needs to make the player **feel them**.

---

# 26. The Design North Star

Every system, scene, interaction and narrative decision should be tested against this:

> **Does this make the player more aware of something they cannot fully explain?**

If yes:

**Keep it.**

If it merely exists because it is a conventional game mechanic:

**Question it.**

If it explains something that would be more powerful if discovered:

**Remove the explanation.**

---

# 27. The Core Experience in One Sentence

> **ÆSITY is a quiet VR science-fiction experience in which maintaining an isolated habitat gradually becomes an investigation into evidence suggesting that the player has already lived the events they are currently experiencing.**

---

# 28. The Ultimate Player Experience

The player should finish ÆSITY and not necessarily say:

> _“That was scary.”_

The ideal response is something closer to:

> **“Wait... what the fuck just happened?”**

Followed by:

> **“I think I understand.”**

And then, ideally:

> **“No. I don't think I do.”**

That is where I think the identity of **ÆSITY** really lives.
