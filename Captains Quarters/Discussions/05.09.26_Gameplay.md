> Source: voice-transcribed working session between Jamie and Rich. ## 1. The Cold Open — now beat-by-beat, and effectively locked

Last session produced the *concept* (dark, a match, no text). This session produced the **shot list**. It's coherent enough to build from directly.

**Beat 1 — Darkness.**
Water lapping against a dinghy hull. Oars working in their rowlocks. Fog. The moon obscured overhead. No HUD, no menu, no prompt.

**Beat 2 — The match.**
Any input — key, gamepad button, mouse click — strikes a match. A lantern at the player's feet comes to life; the player holds it out.

**Beat 3 — The rower revealed.**
The light finds a figure rowing you along — silent. (Species/appearance floated loosely as "an orc or a skeleton"; **treat this as undecided placeholder, not a design decision** — it sits oddly against the grounded Age-of-Sail register established in the land of Romaine.

**Beat 4 — The hook line.**
A gull cries softly in the distance. A faint light shows ahead on a shape in the fog. Music: curiosity with muted excitement. The rower finally speaks — placeholder: *"Rest assured, we will find them. Not today, maybe not tomorrow, but we... will... find them."*
The intent is explicit: the player should think *there is something to uncover*, and it plants the revenge/pursuit thread from Session 1 without exposition.

**Beat 5 — Revelation.**
Music bridges. The shape resolves into a port — lanterns hanging by the jetty, the moon breaking through. The camera swings to frame the structure, cranes upward, and settles on the **title card**.

## 2. The Diegetic Menu — A bold commitment

The core stance, stated repeatedly and forcefully: ** We do not throw a conventional UI menu at the player after that opening.** Menus-for-menus'-sake are a step backwards, we can do better.

### The transition
The camera **holds on the title card** while the next environment loads behind it. Time-of-day is fast-forwarded (via the Ultra Dynamic Sky asset) from night to roughly 8–9am. This would mean that the asset resides at the persistent level. Dockside audio fades in — chatter, gulls, crates being knocked about. Then a coarse, impatient voice: *"You there — wake up, man. Have you paid your mooring fees?"*

The camera then **reverses its own crane path**, descending back down to the jetty to frame the dinghy knocking lazily alongside. The rower is gone. The oars are boated. Only the player character remains, slumped in the stern.

A second shout. The camera closes on the figure and **transitions from cinematic camera into the player camera** — the handover moment, this should feel as seamless as possible. Any input now stands the player up. The player is in control, and no menu has ever appeared. Your new game has begun!

### The menu-as-world problem to be solved
The proposal: the harbourmaster's office contains the "menu" as **interactable objects in the room** rather than screen text. The narrative excuse is already worked out — the harbourmaster takes pity on you ("you don't look well, let me get you a drink"), goes off and **leaves you alone in the room** with time to poke at things.

What actually needs to live there is thinner than expected: no save (the player is already in a new game), so realistically it's **audio/video settings**. Eventually we will need to use the same principle for the equivalent of a *pause menu* etc. I have not yet found a satisfying diegetic metaphor for a these, ideas / suggestions are most welcome!

## 3. World Structure

### The shape
- **Ports are destinations at both ends of a voyage, not a single recurring home base.** Different ports have different offerings and different states. They are all actually places in the world of Romaine, let me know if you need me to provide a map. The one I have mocked up is not 100% complete but it is a fantastic starting point to get us *very* far along.
- **The fog / maelstrom is the run.** Sailing between significant places means crossing it. Entering the fog *is* entering the procedurally generated encounter sequence. Completing the crossing *is* completing the run, and arriving at the destination port is the reward.
- This preserves extraction-style tension (going out is dangerous, arriving is relief) **without** the repetitive home-hub loop we were worried about.

### The starting port
The port you find yourself at is described as *a settlement of wayfarers who have all found their way here by one means or another and have difficulty getting out* — so they make the best of what they've got. This gives the opening location an immediate identity and gives the player a motive (*get out*) before any larger narrative exists.

### The map
CQ is built on **the existing Romaine world map**, with named locations already on it (Ironen, Westport, Prista, Rosalia, Telund's Pipe/"The pipe", a maelstrom or 'Bermuda Triangle' feature at the centre, possibly with a new archipelago or various islands added to suit CQ's needs.

The world map starts mostly undiscovered and opens up as you travel.

## 4. Voyage Traversal

### The mechanic
Within a {fog} crossing, traversal is a **hex map** a rough "3 tall × 5 long" TBD, we will need to test this by feel and play before making it concrete. The player picks which face of the hex to exit through, so routes branch — but **once a direction is chosen, everything behind is locked out.** No backtracking, no free roaming. You're always making forward progress toward the destination, but *which* encounters you meet is your choice.

### The telegraphing question
We were first worried that hidden encounters would be unfair ("bang, surprise, elite enemy — you wouldn't last two minutes"), and leaned toward node-map-style symbols showing what's ahead.

Then we corrected, and the reasoning is worth preserving: **the fog is supposed to be unknown.** Telegraphing individual encounters inside it contradicts its whole identity. The resolution:

- **Inside the fog: shrouded.** You don't see what's on each hex. This is acceptable *because* runs are kept short (a smaller grid, ~3×5 rather than 5×12 for example} — a short run with unknowns is exciting; a long one would be a slog, we never want the game to feel like a chore. We want players excited to go into the unknown. again and again.
- **At the world-map level: informed.** The player chooses *which crossing to attempt* based on a **regional risk heat map** — some routes pass pirate strongholds, some are calmer. High risk, high reward. The information is regional and atmospheric, not itemised ("that area seems dodgy," the way a city street reads dodgy on sight) and explicitly **not in-your-face UI**. We must also bear in mind that the map can and will change over time. There may be a time when there is upset in Westport and it is considered more hostile for example. I believe there is a faction system we can integrate in the world of Romaine. None exists currently so that is a golden opportunity to world-build as Romaine is in fact in its infancy.

**Agency lives at route selection; mystery lives inside the run.**

### Difficulty gating
Two approaches discussed for keeping early players out of deep water: gate map access (black out regions until quests open them), or scale enemy strength to player progression. This needs more consideration and worth discussing further as we flesh things out. 

It is worth noting that the demo should only include three ports. We want to keep things achievable.
### One-use foresight boon
A "magical map" / crystal-ball item granting a brief glimpse of what's on the hexes ahead, then gone — explicitly likened to flipping tiles in a memory game. Small, characterful, and a good fit for a consumable reward. This is something that can possibly be obtained via trade / purchase or lucky discovery. The world of Romaine is full of wonderful opportunities, artifacts and characters.

## 5. Pacing

**Not every leg should be combat.** You wouldn't meet many hostile ships, unless you were sailing in a contested or explicitly dangerous region from A to B. The weights that are placed on the creating a procedural hex map from port to port will take this and several other world factors into consideration together with player-specific data to create unique and interesting encounters that are varied across the player base. More importantly, back-to-back fights turn the crossing into *a slog*.

The prescription:
- Roughly **one or two ship encounters per crossing**, not four or five.
- **Non-combat encounters as deliberate breathing space** — an uncharted island to take on water, food, and timber for repairs; a quiet leg with no encounter at all. This double as a healthy mindfulness segment. I do not want players to feel under constant pressure **to grind** their way through the game. There is adventure and wonder also to be found in the quiet places of Romaine.
- The feeling on arrival should be **curiosity about the next run, not dread of it.**

The generation algorithm should carry **weights** — by region, by world state, by player condition — so encounter density and hostility are tuned rather than uniform random. I mentioned this earlier but worth reiterating as it needs proper attention to bring tastefully into fruition.

**This is a real design constraint, not a nice-to-have.** It NEEDS to be written into the encounter-generation spec directly.

## 6. The Dual Deck

### The proposal
Two decks, used in different contexts:
- **Combat deck** — the existing CQ order/card system, used for ship-to-ship encounters.
- **Exploration / discovery / utility deck** — used for non-combat encounters: going ashore on an uncharted island, investigating, digging, opening things, trading with NPCs even? I do feel like this is a very elegant way to handle a variety of scenarios. I want to make sure this is carefully refined and implemented to determine its feasibility.

Explicitly inspired by a two-deck dungeon crawler *"Shroom & Gloom"* where a shovel card digs mounds, a torch lit dark spaces, and utility cards cracked open chests.

It is critical to remember that at its core this is still a deckbuilder so what you collect / thin will matter.

### The fit
It keeps island, port, town, sea-cave etc. {Places / People} exploration **card-based rather than first-person-adventure-based**, which protects the game's identity as a deckbuilder. The perspective can be first-person, but the player shouldn't be running around with WASD equipping a shovel and swinging it. The *deck* is still how you interact with the world.

Crucially, it makes exploration **subject to deckbuilding decisions**: you might reach an island with a treasure map and discover you **thinned the shovel out of your deck two ports ago**. That's a consequence the player authored themselves — "they can't be angry at how the game played out."

### The critical constraint attached to it
**There must always be more than one way to accomplish a task.** A missing shovel should not be a hard wall ("tough shit, no treasure for you"). The proposed structure maps onto the existing **Force / Focus / Finesse** *tripod mechanism* — the exploration deck carries multiple approaches to the same obstacle, with some cards acting as multi-tools. Digging is the *Force* solution; there should be Focus and Finesse routes to the same outcome.

Also floated: exploration encounters should be **short — five to ten minutes of light exploration**, not sprawling but still meaningful and it should reward curiosity.

## 7. Narrative — the honest state of it

The **overarching narrative and end goal remain undefined**, what's been assembled so far are "fun mechanics" without a spine. 

1. *If the loop is fun enough, players won't care too much about the narrative* — half the battle is won by the mechanics.
2. *BUT a real story would make it exceptional* — "a fun game and great storytelling."

### The modular / emergent approach
Rather than authoring a fixed narrative, build **modular encounters that link back to one another**, so the overarching story is discoverable via many different paths. The player can make different choices in different ports and still uncover the same underlying narrative. We just have to be careful to keep this manageable for a two-man team. It could lead to a dangerous amount of branching dialogue etc. if it is not kept on guard-rails.

Worked example : You rescue someone adrift during a crossing; their home port happens to be your destination; delivering them safely earns a **favour owed**, and that person knows people who can help you. The narrative accumulates through play rather than through cutscenes.

A character with a story you uncover as you go, rather than a rigid quest ladder.

### The quest-chaining structure
The pattern discussed: arrive at a port → pick up a lead there (a wanted board, a tavern contact) → that lead sets your next destination → cross the fog to reach it → arrive with a new thread. Concern has been raised that this risks **locking the player into a quest treadmill**; the modular/tracing-paper approach is the proposed mitigation.

### The tutorial passage
The man who sells you the boat on Rosalia needs passage to the next island. That first crossing is the tutorial: light encounters, low danger, he teaches you the ropes, then leaves after the first port. **It is a tutorial but the player should not feel like it is one.** 

### Still open
**Why is the player going from A to B?** This is honestly left unanswered. It connects both to the missing overarching narrative and to the purpose of the core loop. A mutiny → marooning → revenge spine is compatible and remains a strong candidate. Your thoughts / Ideas / Suggestions?
